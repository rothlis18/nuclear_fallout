In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations exhibit increased densities and biodiversity despite chronic ionizing radiation exposure. This paradox arises from a quantitative trade-off between radiation-induced mortality and the alleviation of anthropogenic pressures. Radiation exposure leads to DNA damage, reduced individual lifespans, and elevated mutation rates, with studies indicating a 1-2% increase in mutation rates per unit of radiation dose. However, the absence of human activities such as agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution results in significant mortality reduction. For instance, in Chernobyl, mammal populations have increased by 30-50% compared to non-contaminated areas, primarily due to the cessation of human disturbances. A population-dynamics model incorporating these factors shows that the net reproductive rate (R0) in these zones can exceed 1, indicating population growth, despite radiation-induced fitness penalties. This model suggests that the reduction in human-induced mortality can outweigh the negative effects of radiation, leading to a net positive impact on wildlife populations. The implications for conservation strategies in similar environments include prioritizing the reduction of human pressures over radiation mitigation, as the former can have a more immediate and substantial positive effect on biodiversity and population dynamics.
