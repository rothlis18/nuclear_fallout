Chronic radiation exposure from ionizing radiation in the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones leads to DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates. The fitness penalty from radiation exposure is quantified by the increase in mutation rates, where a 1 Sv dose of gamma radiation induces a 10^4-fold increase in mutation rates in Drosophila melanogaster (1). In contrast, the mortality reduction from human absence is estimated at 30% in the Chernobyl exclusion zone (2), where the absence of human disturbance and habitat fragmentation allows populations to recover. A population-dynamics assessment modeling net reproductive survival (NRS) can be formulated using the logistic growth equation, where NRS is calculated as NRS = (μ - δ) / (μ + δ), where μ is the intrinsic growth rate and δ is the radiation-induced mortality rate. Assuming a baseline intrinsic growth rate of 0.1 per year and a radiation-induced mortality rate of 0.03 per year, the NRS is 0.97, indicating a 3% increase in population growth rate due to reduced anthropogenic pressures. This suggests that the benefits of reduced human disturbance and habitat fragmentation outweigh the fitness penalty from chronic radiation exposure, with a net reproductive survival rate of 0.97.
