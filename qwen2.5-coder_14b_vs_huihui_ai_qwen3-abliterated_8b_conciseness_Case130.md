In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife thrives despite persistent ionizing radiation, challenging the expectation that chronic radiation would hinder survival. Quantitatively, chronic radiation-induced DNA damage shortens individual lifespans by approximately 10-20% and elevates mutation rates by 1-2%, leading to a net reproductive survival rate reduction of 5-10%. However, the removal of anthropogenic pressures such as habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution significantly enhances survival rates by 30-50%. The net reproductive survival, when comparing the 30-50% increase in survival from reduced human activity to the 5-10% decrease from radiation exposure, results in a net benefit of 20-40%. This mathematical trade-off suggests that in similar environments, conservation and management strategies should prioritize reducing human impacts over concerns about low-level radiation exposure, as the former provides a more substantial benefit to wildlife populations.
