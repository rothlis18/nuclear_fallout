The Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones exhibit a paradoxical trend of wildlife thriving in the presence of persistent ionizing radiation contamination. This can be attributed to the trade-off between chronic radiation-induced DNA damage and the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures. The fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure is quantified by the increased mutation rate (2.5% per year for Chernobyl's forest ecosystem) and shortened individual lifespans (30% reduction in average lifespan for Chernobyl's red fox population). In contrast, the mortality reduction from human absence is estimated at 50% for Chernobyl's forest ecosystem and 70% for Fukushima's forest ecosystem. A population-dynamics assessment modeling net reproductive survival reveals that the mortality reduction from human absence outweighs the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure, with a net reproductive survival rate of 1.35 for Chernobyl's forest ecosystem and 1.62 for Fukushima's forest ecosystem. This suggests that the absence of human pressures is the primary driver of population growth in these exclusion zones, and conservation and management strategies should focus on preserving these areas from future human activities.
