In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations have demonstrated unexpected resilience, thriving amidst persistent ionizing radiation contamination. This phenomenon can be quantitatively assessed through a population-dynamics model that weighs the costs of chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates against the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures. The primary mechanism of survival enhancement is the absence of human activities such as habitat fragmentation, intensive agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution, which typically lead to species decline. For instance, in the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone, certain species like the European bison and wild boar have shown population growth rates comparable to those in control areas, suggesting a net reproductive survival benefit from reduced human pressures. However, the radiation exposure imposes a fitness penalty, with estimated mutation rates in some species increasing by up to 20% due to chronic radiation exposure. This genetic load may lead to a 15% reduction in effective population size over generations, increasing the risk of extinction from genetic drift. Despite these challenges, the overall population trend indicates that the benefits of reduced human impact outweigh the immediate costs of radiation exposure, at least in the short to medium term. This paradox highlights the need for conservation strategies that balance the preservation of these unique ecosystems with the long-term genetic health of the species inhabiting them.
