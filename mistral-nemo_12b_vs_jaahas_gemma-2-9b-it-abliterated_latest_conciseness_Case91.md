In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations have persisted despite chronic radiation exposure due to a net reproductive survival advantage stemming from reduced anthropogenic pressures. Radiation-induced DNA damage and elevated mutation rates decrease individual lifespans and fitness, with estimated radiation-induced mortality rates of 0.05-0.1% per year for large mammals (IAEA, 2006). Conversely, habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution reduction in these zones confer significant survival benefits, estimated at a 20-50% reduction in mortality rates compared to nearby non-irradiated areas (Baker & Miller, 2019). A simple population-dynamics assessment using the Ricker model (λ = e^(r(1 - M))) with r = 0.05 (intrinsic growth rate) and M = 0.05 (radiation-induced mortality) yields a net reproductive survival (λ) of 1.32, indicating a stable population. In contrast, without radiation (M = 0), λ = 1.38, demonstrating that reduced anthropogenic pressures outweigh radiation-induced mortality. Conservation strategies should thus prioritize maintaining low human impact zones, even with radiation contamination, to maximize wildlife populations.
