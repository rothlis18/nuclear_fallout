The Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones present a paradox where wildlife thrives despite persistent ionizing radiation contamination. The primary mechanism of this phenomenon is the reduction of anthropogenic pressures, including habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution. In contrast, chronic radiation exposure imposes a fitness penalty through chronic DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates. A quantitative assessment can be modeled using the Leslie matrix, which describes population dynamics in terms of birth and death rates. For example, in Chernobyl, the increase in red deer populations by a factor of 5.6 and roe deer by a factor of 3.2 since the disaster can be attributed to reduced human presence. However, the effective reproductive number (R0) for these species in the exclusion zone is lower than in control areas due to higher mortality rates from radiation exposure. The trade-off between these factors can be quantified by comparing the mortality reduction from human absence (e.g., 50% reduction in human-caused mortality) to the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure (e.g., 20% reduction in reproductive rate). This analysis suggests that while radiation remains a significant threat, the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures can outweigh the costs in certain species and environments, offering insights for conservation strategies in similar contexts.
