**Analysis:**

1. **Radiation Exposure and DNA Damage:**
   - The average radiation dose in the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone (CEZ) is ~1 mSv/year, and in the Fukushima Exclusion Zone (FEZ) is ~0.24 mSv/year, comparable to natural background radiation (~0.3 mSv/year).
   - DNA damage occurs at ~0.05 Gy (50 rads), but chronic low-dose radiation (<0.1 Gy/year) may not significantly impact survival or reproduction in wildlife due to DNA repair mechanisms and potential adaptive responses.

2. **Anthropogenic Pressures:**
   - Habitat fragmentation reduces population connectivity, leading to inbreeding, reduced genetic diversity, and increased extinction risk.
   - Agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution cause direct mortality, habitat destruction, and ecosystem degradation.

3. **Population-Dynamics Assessment:**
   - Let's define:
     - \( R_0 \) as the net reproductive rate (number of female offspring per female per generation).
     - \( \mu_r \) as the radiation-induced mortality rate.
     - \( \mu_a \) as the anthropogenic mortality rate.
     - \( \mu \) as the total mortality rate.
     - \( \lambda \) as the population growth rate.
   - The net reproductive rate can be modeled as: \( R_0 = \frac{b}{a + \mu_r + \mu_a} \), where \( b \) is the birth rate.
   - Assuming \( \mu_r \) is proportional to radiation dose (\( D \)): \( \mu_r = k \cdot D \), where \( k \) is a constant.
   - Without radiation, the net reproductive rate would be: \( R_0' = \frac{b}{a + \mu_a} \).

4. **Modeling and Implications:**
   - A higher \( \mu_a \) reduces \( R_0 \), but a higher \( \mu_r \) also reduces \( R_0 \). The optimal scenario is when \( \mu_r \) is low enough that the reduction in \( \mu_a \) leads to a higher \( R_0 \).
   - Using data from CEZ and FEZ, we can estimate \( k \) and compare \( R_0 \) and \( R_0' \) to determine if wildlife populations are indeed thriving due to reduced anthropogenic pressures.
   - If \( R_0 > R_0' \), then reduced anthropogenic pressures outweigh radiation-induced mortality, supporting the observed wildlife thriving.

**Implications for Conservation and Management Strategies:**
- In similar environments, prioritize reducing anthropogenic pressures over radiation concerns, as wildlife appear to benefit more from reduced human activity.
- Monitor radiation levels and wildlife populations to ensure no significant radiation-induced declines occur over time.
- Consider managed exclusion zones for conservation purposes, balancing radiation risks with anthropogenic pressure reductions.
- Conduct further research to refine the model and better understand species-specific responses to chronic low-dose radiation.
