Wildlife in the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones thrives due to reduced anthropogenic pressures such as habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution, outweighing the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure. Studies indicate that while chronic ionizing radiation induces DNA damage, leading to shortened individual lifespans and elevated mutation rates, the net reproductive survival of species is enhanced. For instance, in Chernobyl, populations of wolves, elk, and other large mammals have increased by 260% since the 1986 disaster. Similarly, in Fukushima, bird populations have surged by 56% in the exclusion zone compared to unaffected areas. Quantitatively, the reduction in human-induced mortality from the absence of hunting, poaching, and habitat destruction significantly outweighs the radiation-induced fitness costs. This suggests that in similar environments, conservation and management strategies should prioritize minimizing human impact over concerns about radiation exposure, as the latter's effects are outweighed by the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures.
