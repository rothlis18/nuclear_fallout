In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife thrives due to reduced anthropogenic pressures, including habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution, outweighing the chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened individual lifespans, and elevated mutation rates. Quantitatively, the net reproductive survival can be modeled using the equation \( R_0 = \frac{b}{d} \), where \( b \) is the birth rate and \( d \) is the death rate. In these zones, the death rate \( d \) decreases by 30% due to the absence of human activities, while the birth rate \( b \) increases by 20% due to improved habitat quality. Thus, \( R_0 \) increases from 1.0 to 1.26, indicating a 26% increase in population growth rate. This suggests that the benefits of reduced human activity significantly enhance net reproductive survival, making these areas valuable for conservation and management strategies in similar environments.
