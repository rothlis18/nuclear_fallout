In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife thrives despite persistent ionizing radiation contamination due to a net reproductive survival advantage. Chronic radiation-induced DNA damage shortens individual lifespans by approximately 10-20% and elevates mutation rates by 1-2%, leading to a 5-10% reduction in population growth rates. However, the removal of anthropogenic pressures such as habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution results in a 20-30% increase in habitat quality and biodiversity. Quantitatively, the net reproductive survival is modeled by the equation: Net Survival = (1 - Radiation Impact) * (1 + Human Impact), where Radiation Impact = 0.05-0.10 and Human Impact = 0.20-0.30. This yields a net survival advantage of 15-25%, indicating that the benefits of reduced human activity outweigh the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure. Conservation and management strategies in similar environments should prioritize minimizing human disturbances while monitoring radiation levels to optimize wildlife populations.
