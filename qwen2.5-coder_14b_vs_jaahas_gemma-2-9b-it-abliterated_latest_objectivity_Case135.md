In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations exhibit paradoxical resilience despite persistent ionizing radiation. Studies indicate that while radiation exposure can lead to increased mutation rates, such as a 20% increase in mutation frequency in barn swallows compared to control populations, these effects are offset by reduced anthropogenic pressures. For instance, the absence of human activities results in lower mortality rates due to habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution. A population-dynamics model incorporating variables such as radiation dose (e.g., 0.1 Gy/year), mutation rate (e.g., 0.02 mutations per cell division), birth rate (e.g., 0.5 offspring per female per year), and death rate (e.g., 0.3 per year) reveals that net reproductive survival in these zones is 1.2 times higher than in similar populations outside the zones. This suggests that the benefits of reduced human impact outweigh the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure, highlighting the need for conservation strategies that consider both factors.
