In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations have thrived despite persistent ionizing radiation contamination. The trade-off between chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates versus reduced anthropogenic pressures is quantifiable. For example, in Chernobyl's 30-km exclusion zone, certain species like the European bison and wild boar have population densities comparable to those in less contaminated areas. This is attributed to a 70-80% reduction in human-caused mortality factors, such as hunting and habitat fragmentation, offsetting the estimated 2-5% annual genetic mutation rate increase due to radiation exposure. Modeling these dynamics, a reproductive survival rate 15-25% higher than in control areas can be projected, assuming a baseline 10-year lifespan reduction. This suggests that in environments where human pressures are significantly reduced, the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure can be outweighed by the benefits of habitat preservation, leading to net positive population growth. This phenomenon has implications for conservation strategies, indicating that areas contaminated by radiation might serve as refuges for species threatened by human activities, provided proper management strategies are implemented to mitigate direct radiation effects on wildlife.
