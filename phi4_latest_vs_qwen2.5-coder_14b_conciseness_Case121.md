In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations exhibit paradoxical resilience despite chronic ionizing radiation exposure. The mathematical model of population dynamics considers two primary forces: the reduction in mortality due to decreased anthropogenic pressures (e.g., habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution) and the fitness penalty from radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates. Quantitatively, the absence of human activity can lead to a mortality reduction of up to 50%, while radiation exposure may increase individual mortality rates by 10-20% and reduce reproductive success by 5-15%. The net reproductive rate (R0) in these zones can be modeled as R0 = (1 - M_human) * (1 - M_radiation) * B, where M_human and M_radiation are mortality reductions from human absence and radiation, respectively, and B is the baseline reproductive rate. This model suggests that the net effect of reduced human presence can outweigh the negative impacts of radiation, leading to stable or even increasing population sizes. Implications for conservation strategies include prioritizing the protection of large, contiguous habitats from human encroachment and considering the potential for similar environments to serve as refuges for wildlife in the face of increasing anthropogenic pressures.
