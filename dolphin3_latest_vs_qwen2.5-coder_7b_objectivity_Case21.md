In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations have demonstrated unexpected resilience, thriving amidst persistent ionizing radiation contamination. The mathematical trade-off between chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates versus the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures can be quantitatively assessed through population-dynamics modeling. For instance, in Chernobyl, studies have shown that while radiation exposure leads to increased mutation rates and reduced lifespans, the absence of human activities has significantly lowered mortality rates from habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution. The net reproductive survival rate of species in these zones can be modeled by comparing the mortality reduction from human absence to the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure. For example, in Chernobyl, certain species like the European bison have shown a net reproductive survival rate of approximately 2.5 offspring per female per year, despite a potential 20-30% reduction in lifespan due to radiation exposure. This rate is higher than in control populations outside the exclusion zones, indicating that the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures outweigh the costs of radiation exposure for these species. Such assessments provide valuable insights for conservation and management strategies in similar environments, emphasizing the need to balance the risks of radiation exposure with the benefits of reduced human impact.
