In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations exhibit increased densities and biodiversity despite chronic ionizing radiation exposure, a phenomenon driven by a mathematical trade-off between radiation-induced mortality and reduced anthropogenic pressures. Ionizing radiation causes DNA damage, leading to increased mutation rates and reduced individual lifespans, quantified by a dose-dependent increase in mortality rates and a decrease in reproductive success. For instance, studies in Chernobyl show a 1.5-2 fold increase in mutation rates in certain species. However, the absence of human activities such as agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution results in significant habitat recovery and reduced mortality from these sources. Quantitative models, such as those using Leslie matrices, demonstrate that the net reproductive rate (R0) can remain above one in these environments due to decreased human-induced mortality, despite radiation-induced fitness penalties. For example, in Chernobyl, bird populations have increased by 30% compared to non-exclusion areas, attributed to a 50% reduction in human-caused mortality. These findings suggest that in similar environments, conservation strategies should prioritize minimizing human disturbances to leverage the resilience of wildlife populations, even in the presence of chronic radiation exposure.
