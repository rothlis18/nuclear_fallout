The paradox of wildlife thriving in the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones can be explained by the mathematical trade-off between chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened individual lifespans, and elevated mutation rates, versus the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures. Chronic radiation exposure leads to increased mutation rates and higher rates of cancer, reducing individual lifespans. However, the absence of human activity in these zones has led to reduced habitat fragmentation, decreased agricultural and industrial pollution, and reduced hunting pressure. These factors have allowed wildlife populations to thrive, with some species experiencing population increases. A quantitative population-dynamics assessment modeling net reproductive survival shows that the mortality reduction from human absence outweighs the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure, leading to net reproductive survival. This has significant implications for conservation and management strategies in similar environments, highlighting the importance of balancing ecological restoration with radiation safety.
