In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife populations have demonstrated remarkable resilience, thriving despite the presence of persistent ionizing radiation contamination. The mathematical trade-off between chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates versus the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures can be assessed through a quantitative population-dynamics model. The model should account for the reduction in mortality due to human absence and the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure. For instance, in Chernobyl, certain species like the European bison and wild boar have shown population growth rates comparable to those in control areas, suggesting that the benefits of reduced human pressure outweigh the costs of radiation exposure. A key mechanism is the reduction in habitat fragmentation, allowing for larger home ranges and reduced competition for resources. Additionally, the absence of agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution has led to a more stable and diverse ecosystem. To quantify this, consider a simple Leslie matrix model, where the net reproductive survival rate (R0) is calculated as the product of survival probabilities and fertility rates. For a species with a baseline R0 of 2.0, a 20% reduction in survival due to radiation exposure could be offset by a 25% increase in fertility rates, resulting in a stable population. This analysis underscores the importance of considering both the detrimental effects of radiation and the ecological benefits of reduced human activity when developing conservation strategies for these unique environments.
