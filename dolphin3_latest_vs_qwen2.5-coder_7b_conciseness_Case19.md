The Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones present a paradox where wildlife thrives despite persistent ionizing radiation contamination. The mathematical trade-off involves chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened lifespans, and elevated mutation rates, weighed against reduced anthropogenic pressures such as habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution. In these areas, human absence leads to a significant reduction in mortality rates for wildlife, offsetting the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure. For instance, at Chernobyl, studies have shown that certain species, like the European bison and wild boar, have established stable populations with mutation rates slightly higher than background levels but not significantly impacting their reproductive success. Modeling these dynamics, a 20-30% reduction in reproductive success due to radiation exposure can be balanced by a 50% reduction in mortality rates, leading to a net increase in population growth. This assessment suggests that conservation strategies in similar environments should focus on leveraging the benefits of reduced human impact while mitigating the effects of radiation exposure through habitat management and species-specific interventions.
