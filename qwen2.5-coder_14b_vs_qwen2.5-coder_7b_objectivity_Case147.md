Wildlife in the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones thrives due to the reduction in anthropogenic pressures such as habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution, outweighing the chronic radiation-induced DNA damage, shortened individual lifespans, and elevated mutation rates. Studies indicate that while radiation exposure can lead to increased mutation rates and decreased survival rates, the absence of human activities has resulted in a net reproductive survival advantage. For instance, in Chernobyl, populations of certain species, like wolves and deer, have increased by up to 260% and 150%, respectively, since the exclusion zone was established. Similarly, in Fukushima, bird populations have shown significant growth. Quantitatively, the reduction in human-induced mortality from factors like poaching and habitat destruction can be modeled to have a greater impact on population dynamics than the fitness penalty from radiation exposure. This suggests that in similar environments, conservation and management strategies should prioritize minimizing human interference to enhance biodiversity, despite the presence of ionizing radiation.
