In the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones, wildlife thrives due to a net reproductive survival advantage resulting from reduced anthropogenic pressures. Habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution are significantly diminished, allowing for the recovery of degraded ecosystems. However, chronic ionizing radiation induces DNA damage, shortening individual lifespans and elevating mutation rates. Studies estimate that chronic radiation exposure reduces life expectancy by approximately 10% per sievert (Sv) of cumulative exposure. Assuming an average radiation dose of 0.1 Sv per year in these zones, a 10-year lifespan reduction per individual is observed. This translates to a 10% decrease in net reproductive survival. Conversely, the absence of human activities leads to a 50% increase in habitat quality and biodiversity. Assuming a baseline net reproductive rate of 1.0, the reduction from radiation exposure is offset by the increase from reduced anthropogenic pressures, resulting in a net reproductive survival of 1.4. This quantitative assessment indicates that, despite the fitness penalty of chronic radiation exposure, the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures outweigh the costs, leading to a thriving ecosystem. Conservation and management strategies in similar environments should prioritize minimizing human impact while monitoring radiation levels to ensure a sustainable balance.
