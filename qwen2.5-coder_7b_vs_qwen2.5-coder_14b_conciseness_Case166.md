The paradox of wildlife thriving in the Chernobyl and Fukushima exclusion zones can be explained by the trade-off between chronic radiation-induced DNA damage and the benefits of reduced anthropogenic pressures. Chronic radiation exposure leads to increased mutation rates and shortened individual lifespans, reducing reproductive survival. However, the absence of human activity significantly reduces habitat fragmentation, agriculture, hunting, and industrial pollution, which are major threats to wildlife. Assuming a baseline mortality rate of 50% due to radiation and a 30% reduction in anthropogenic pressures, the net reproductive survival could increase by 20%. This trade-off suggests that conservation efforts should focus on mitigating radiation exposure while preserving the reduced anthropogenic pressures to maintain wildlife populations.
